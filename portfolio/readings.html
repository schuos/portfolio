Reading Responses

Ch. 1, “In Plato’s Cave”, of On Photography, by Susan Sontag - The Use of Photography

If I look at the earliest months in my phone’s photo gallery, I would see a period when I took photos only when I had to. I took photos for school, or group photos that I shared if I spent time with friends or family. In the book, On Photography, written by Susan Sontag, a writer on modern culture, she compares photography to Plato’s Cave. Similar to the shadows the prisoners see, we see photos, capturing a fragment of reality. While to create a photo is to use a camera and capture what is in front of the photographer, it is not limited to solely depict what is in front of them, but free to manipulate its interpretation through intention. In a society where the average citizen has access to a camera, photography is normalized and is now part of everyday life.

In the chapter “In Plato’s Cave,” Sontag emphasizes the excessiveness of photography in everyday life. She describes it as being “unsure of other responses" and to “appease the anxiety which the work-driven feel about not working when they are on vacation” (Sontag 10). It is an “aesthetic consumerism” and a “form of mental pollution” (Sontag 24). These point to wanting a moment to last forever, achieved through taking a photo, to prove that it should not be forgotten. In my media course, we discussed the advancement of communication in humanity, from word of mouth to writing. The philosopher, Socrates, believed that writing would weaken one’s memory. Sontag and Socrates acknowledge advancements in daily life could have consequences. Photography has become an option at every instance, from dishes eaten at a restaurant to experiencing golden sunsets. This creates the question of is someone fully experiencing the present when all they are thinking about is taking a picture, and how much thought is actually put into taking a picture?

However, one could argue there is significance in capturing little moments in life. Taking a picture with the intention to highlight little moments in order to appreciate it, makes it more meaningful. During freshman English class, I participated in an exercise where I recalled sounds and sights while commuting to campus to practice mindfulness. Being aware of the birds that chirp and the chatter between customers and the man who runs the food cart keeps my mind away from the exam I have later. There would be fondness and acknowledgement behind the photos I could take of these moments. I believe that the skill of being mindful and noticing the details around me will help in representing these moments visually, not just by understanding what subject to focus on, but how I can fully depict what I am looking at means to me.

Unlike the past, I have started to take more photos because I want to. Many times, the raw photo lacks thought and looks different from what I am seeing. My personal goal is to be able to utilize the environment to capture what I am seeing and feeling.

Works Cited: - Sontag, Susan. On Photography. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 1977

Your Brain on ChatGPT

AI use today is a somewhat controversial topic. I do believe that using AI is beneficial as a study tool. For example, for practice test questions and explaining concepts in a simpler form. Based on the reading and the podcast assigned, they both explain the detriments of using AI to replace critical thinking when for example, writing a paper. I think that there is a difference between using AI as a tool and using AI as a means to replace human thinking. Ultimately there should be clearer guidelines for the use of AI, and that it should not be used to replace human thought and creativity, especially if this is how it is being used by younger generations. This reflection will be about my thoughts while reading the article and watching the podcast.

The experiment in the article titled "Your Brain on ChatGPT", by Nataliya Kosmyna, finds that people who used an LLM or Large Language Model the first time and switched to using their brain the second time, showed "weaker neural connectivity" and was unable to quote their essays, while those who used their brain before switching to an LLM the second time showed "higher memory recall" (Kosmyna). Because some of the findings are related to the concept of whether memory is affected with the use of AI, I questioned if memory seemed to have decreased because of the lack of engagement when doing tasks. In a boot camp for my anatomy class, I learned that engaging more of my senses made terms easier to memorize. For example, removing the typing aspect of writing with your fingers and hands because instead, an LLM is generating an essay for you. The sense of touch would be excluded when doing this task.

Something that stood out to me in the podcast "Brain Experts WARNING; Watch this Before Using ChatGPT Again!", uploaded by The Diary of a CEO, is when Dr. Daniel Amens states that Generation Z is "the sickest generation in the world's history," because of the "unleash of the cellphone and social media" (The Diary of a CEO, 37:30 - 37:54). I thought that this was too much of a generalization when speaking about how "58% of teenage girls report feeling persistently sad" and "32% have thought about killing themselves" (The Diary of a CEO, 37:30 - 37:54). While the cell phone and social media is a large factor of our lives, I would argue that these statistics are about something much more serious than the use of a cellphone. The cellphone and social media acts more like a messenger who communicates about the current state of the world. While beauty standards are much easier to reach us, we are now also much more aware of issues such as global warming, police brutality, and the constant complaints on the state of the job market. While one can argue that if we never had cellphones, these statistics would not be as high, all the bigger issues will not disappear. Saying that technology is the sole reason why the generation is unhappy is an understatement.

Overall, I understood the concern for the lack of action towards regulating the use of technology, or improving systems for the better good of human thinking. The lack of concern and initiative over internet safety from many tech companies is significant because they have that direct power to change the service. Ultimately, it is easier to change the policies of the technology corporations are controlling compared to trying to alter the day to day decisions of users.

Graphic Novel: Motherhood in Crisis - Chapter 1: Memunatu

A story can be depicted in different ways. In ancient Greece, stories were shared purely through word of mouth. Today, we see stories being depicted not just through speaking, but also with the use of art, photography, sound, and text. In chapter 1 of the graphic novel, Motherhood in Crisis, made by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager, Saidu Bah, Memunatu, a woman who shares her story on losing her child and the journey of recovering from obstetric fistula, an opening forming due to prolonged labor between the birth canal and the bladder. Through the use of illustration, text, photography and video, these elements depict the unrecorded and the devastating reality of a population in Sierra Leone, where inaccessibility of healthcare correlates to child stillbirth and untreated complications of mothers after labor.

In the graphic novel, illustration depicts Memunatu’s feelings of shame and heartbreak after losing her child. Text reveals her thoughts of seeing her deceased child as unbearable, illustrations depict the changes in her body as she refused to leave her bed. The same composition and pose is used in two frames, one showing Memunatu losing weight. The artist uses light and shadow to depict Memunatu’s shame, as she hides behind trees, observing the other women who wore bright and colorful clothing, in which she felt they were happy unlike her. These depictions convey Memunatu’s struggle in adapting to daily life after losing her child.

At the end of the chapter, photos and video are shown instead of illustration, showing the current instead of the past, as using images taken from real life to bring readers back to the current reality and the present for Memunatu, who has successfully delivered a baby, in a healthcare facility instead of at home with who she described as the “lie lie nurse.” The photos show Memunatu in the facility with her baby and husband, now smiling. For Memunatu to reveal her actual appearance and of her family makes the chapter more personal because the story she shares is vulnerable. It also emphasizes that this issue is real, when illustrations could detach the viewer.

In the chapter, illustration was used primarily as a means of storytelling, whereas photography was primarily used to showcase the significance of accessibility to healthcare. If Memunatu continued not having the care she needed, the photos in the end would not be possible. If this story was text only, readers would not fully understand the severity of what Memunatu experienced or of the visual cues. For example, the illustrations depicted Memnuatu regaining the weight she had lost during her time at the facility and the interactions displayed between her and the workers show that she was supported and did not feel ashamed during her time there. Without the visuals, writers would have to fill in these gaps.

Through the use of illustration and photography and video, the chapter spreads awareness on an ongoing issue women face, according to the World Health Organization, in parts of Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, due to for example, a lack of access to healthcare. By using Memunatu’s positive experience of receiving treatment, there is hope and support is needed.
